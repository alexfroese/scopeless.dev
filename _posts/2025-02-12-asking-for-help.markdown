# Asking for help

## The Problem

One thing I've noticed a lot of devs do, and I've been guilty of it too, is that when they ask for
help, it's often from a point where they already have a solution in mind, they just don't know how
to accomplish it. Sometimes they're even so far down their rabbit hole that they're asking
questions about problems they've created that could've been avoided.

## How we handled it

Because of my expectations of others, I have a tendency to answer the question I'm asked. This
often ends up with me giving advice that helps the dev accomplish their goal, even if their
solution needs some work. In the particular case I have in mind, the dev only gave as much context
as was needed to make their solution sound reasonably plausible, but as we got closer to their
imagined solution, something felt off to me. That was my hint to take a few steps back and look at
the actual objective. Turns out there was an easier solution all along.

## Anything we could've done better?

I think we handled this situation pretty well, all things considered. Sometimes I worry about what
happens in those cases where I don't get the itch to take a few steps back before getting to their
questions, but in this case everything happened the way it was supposed to, generally.

## Any advice for avoiding this entirely?

When asking a question, start from the beginning. I love hearing about proposed solutions, it shows
initiative and allows devs to stretch their problem solving skills. But in order for me to properly
guide less experienced devs properly, I need to know the problem we're working on first. Maybe
their solution is a good one, in which case I'm happy to help them work out the pieces they're
missing.

## Anything I personally could do better in these situations?

Sometimes I have a habit of giving away the answer instead of trying to lead devs to it their own
way. I like the Socratic method, because I feel like it's a better way to have anyone learn
anything, really. It's the closest thing I can think of to teaching thought patterns, which are way
more valuable long-term than straight-forward answers now.
